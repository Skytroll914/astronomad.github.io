# Astronaut Stress, Depression & Anxiety Assessment Protocol

Assessing astronaut mental health requires adapting validated clinical psychometrics for **Isolated, Confined, and Extreme (ICE)** environments—where crew time is tightly scheduled, microgravity mimics physical symptoms of mood disorders, and high-achieving crew members are culturally inclined to underreport distress.

---

## 1. How to Perform the Assessment

Effective spaceflight behavioral monitoring relies on four operational protocols rather than a one-off terrestrial clinic form:

* **Use a Baseline-Delta Model:** Administer the questionnaire at pre-flight milestones (e.g., 90, 30, and 7 days before launch) to establish each astronaut’s personal baseline. In orbit, deviations from an individual's own baseline (delta) are far more diagnostic than population-level clinical cutoffs.
* **Separate Somatic Noise from Affective Symptoms:** Standard depression and anxiety scales rely heavily on somatic items—fatigue, sleep disruption, appetite shifts, and slowed movement. In microgravity, circadian misalignment, fluid shifts, and heavy workloads cause these symptoms in healthy astronauts. Weight cognitive and emotional items (anhedonia, hopelessness, uncontrollable worry) higher than physical ones.
* **Counter "Right Stuff" Underreporting:** Astronauts may minimize symptoms out of concern for flight status or team perception. Frame the assessment around **operational readiness, cognitive load, and recovery**, pair symptom items with neutral mood-adjective checklists (such as the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule, or PANAS), and route responses via encrypted, private crew tablets directly to flight surgeons or behavioral health specialists.
* **Match Cadence to Mission Phase:** Use rapid weekly check-ins during routine operations, increasing frequency around high-stress phases (docking, EVAs, mission extensions, or communication blackouts).

---

## 2. How Many Questions It Should Take

Crew schedules rarely tolerate lengthy surveys. Space agencies and analog missions (such as NASA's HERA and ISS behavioral health protocols) balance psychometric reliability against survey fatigue using a two-tier structure:

| Assessment Tier | Question Count | Completion Time | Frequency | Psychometric Basis |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Routine Operational Check-In** | **16 questions** | ~2 minutes | Weekly | Short-form cores of **PSS-4**, **PHQ-9**, and **GAD-7** + ICE crew context |
| **Comprehensive Milestone Screen** | **26–30 questions** | ~5–6 minutes | Monthly / Pre- & Post-Flight | Full **PHQ-9** (9 items) + **GAD-7** (7 items) + **PSS-10** (10 items) + sleep/cohesion items |

---

## 3. Sample 16-Item Astronaut Questionnaire

This weekly operational instrument captures **Stress**, **Depression**, **Anxiety**, and **ICE-specific environmental friction** while filtering out routine microgravity side effects.

**Instructions:** *Over the past 7 days, how often have you experienced the following? Rate each item from **0** to **3**:*  
**0** = Not at all | **1** = On 1–2 days | **2** = More than half the days | **3** = Nearly every day

### Part A: Perceived & Operational Stress (Adapted from PSS-4)
1. Felt that mission tasks or unexpected anomalies were piling up faster than you could comfortably manage.
2. Felt unable to control the key factors affecting your daily schedule or work performance.
3. Felt confident in your ability to handle personal or operational problems as they arose. *(Reverse-scored: 3 = 0, 2 = 1, 1 = 2, 0 = 3)*
4. Felt that things were going your way and mission demands felt predictable. *(Reverse-scored)*

### Part B: Mood & Depression Screen (Adapted from PHQ-9, Cognitive/Affective Focus)
5. Had little interest, satisfaction, or pleasure in doing things you normally enjoy—including off-duty hobbies, Earth observation, or crew meals.
6. Felt down, emotionally flat, or discouraged about the mission or your personal situation.
7. Felt like you were letting your crewmates, ground team, or family down, or felt overly critical of your own performance.
8. Had trouble concentrating on complex procedures, checklists, or reading—even when well-rested.

### Part C: Anxiety & Hyperarousal Screen (Adapted from GAD-7)
9. Felt nervous, keyed up, or constantly on edge, even during routine operations.
10. Found yourself unable to stop or control worrying about potential system failures, mission outcomes, or events back on Earth.
11. Had trouble relaxing or mentally "switching off" during scheduled off-duty or sleep periods.
12. Become easily annoyed, impatient, or irritable over minor procedural or environmental inconveniences.

### Part D: ICE Environment & Crew Cohesion Context
13. Felt a desire to withdraw socially or avoid non-essential interaction with fellow crew members.
14. Experienced interpersonal friction, miscommunication, or unresolved tension with crewmates or Mission Control.
15. Felt mentally unrefreshed upon waking, independent of noise, lighting, or physical discomfort in your sleep station.
16. Felt emotionally detached or isolated from family and support networks on Earth.

## 4. Scoring & Triage Thresholds

* **Total Score Range:** `0` to `48` (each subscale scores `0` to `12`).
* **Green (0–11 total, or within +3 points of personal baseline):** Normal operational adaptation; no intervention required.
* **Yellow (12–21 total, or +4 to +7 point jump from baseline):** Elevated strain. Triggers low-friction countermeasures—adjusting workload distribution, scheduling an extra private family video conference, or offering self-guided cognitive behavioral modules.
* **Red (22+ total, +8 point jump from baseline, or a score of 2+ on items 5, 6, or 13):** Clinical review threshold. Initiates a confidential tele-psychology consultation with the flight surgeon or behavioral health officer.
